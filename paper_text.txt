an all-or-nothing yield pattern and non-zero leadtimes

A B S T R A C T
We consider the single-item, periodic-review inventory system with stochastic demand, an all-or-nothing 
random yield pattern, and non-zero leadtimes, where the unmet demand is backlogged. The firm’s objective 
is to find an optimal ordering policy to minimize its total expected discounted cost. As the optimal policy 
remains unknown, we first develop a new heuristic that takes advantage of the all-or-nothing yield pattern and 
then propose a deep reinforcement learning (DRL) algorithm based on a tailored neural network model. The 
proposed DRL algorithm combines the results of existing heuristics to improve performance and stabilize the 
training process. Moreover, the total cost to be computed is convex in the decision variable, which facilitates 
the search. Extensive numerical experiments show that our new solution methods outperform known heuristics 
and demonstrate near-optimal performance when leadtimes are relatively short and the minimal cost can be 
achieved numerically.
1. Introduction
This research is motivated by one of the co-authors’ project expe-
riences at a pharmaceutical firm specializing in antibiotic drugs that 
requires a framework for replenishing procured key ingredients/ma-
terials. During its fulfilling process, active ingredients and auxiliary 
materials (e.g., starch and capsules) mixed or in direct contact with 
drugs can cause quality risks. These incoming material shipments must 
be sampled to screen out microbial contamination. Thus, the firm 
conducts a sample test immediately upon the arrival of each order 
shipment before production use. The entire shipment will be disposed 
of or returned if the sample test fails. This yield characteristic is referred 
to as an all-or-nothing yield pattern. The all-or-nothing type of yield 
is especially relevant in biomanufacturing due to random shocks that 
can affect the entire production (Tomlin, 2009; Martagan et al., 2016, 
2020). For example, during the freeze-drying process, physico-chemical 
process parameters (i.e., temperature, moisture, PH, etc.) might deviate 
from their pre-specified control ranges due to bacterial contamination, 
human errors, or equipment failure.  In such cases, the whole shipment 
needs to be scrapped to comply with regulatory requirements after 
receiving the shipment.
As a niche player in a high-end market, the pharmaceutical firm 
used to enjoy a healthy profit margin. However, as new competitors 
entered the market, the management began to pay more attention to 
its manufacturing operations and supply chain processes. For example,

With the above motivational examples, the current paper consid-
ers the single-item, periodic-review inventory system with stochastic 
demand, an all-or-nothing random yield pattern, and non-zero lead-
times. The objective is to find an efficient inventory replenishment 
policy that tries to minimize the expected discounted cost, including 
purchasing, inventory-holding, and backlogging costs over the planning 
horizon. As well understood, the structure of an optimal inventory 
control policy for such a problem is likely to be complex due to the 
intrinsic challenges of problems with an all-or-nothing random yield 
model and leadtimes. On the one hand, this inventory system must 
cope with uncertainties from both the demand and production sides, 
facing the possibility that some order shipments in transit may become 
non-usable. On the other hand, the dynamic programming (DP) model 
of this problem also suffers from the curse of dimensionality, which

of the optimal solution’s structure, exact numerical methods, such as 
value and policy improvement iteration algorithms (Puterman, 2014),

Although several heuristics have been proposed and tested under 
a general form of yield randomness, only a few are known for the 
problem with an all-or-nothing yield pattern. Past studies (Inderfurth, 
2009; Inderfurth and Vogelgesang, 2013; Inderfurth and Kiesmüller, 
2015) have developed a linear inflation rule (LIR), where orders are 
placed only when inventory positions are below certain target inven-
tory levels. If the gap between the target level and the current inventory 
position is positive, then the order quantity is set equal to the difference 
multiplied by the reciprocal of the mean yield rate. Although this rule 
considers demand and yield uncertainty when calculating target stock 
levels, it is too simple to consider yield variability when determining 
order quantities. Instead of using the inverse of the mean yield rate as a 
yield inflation factor, Chen et al. (2018) propose a weighted newsven-
dor heuristic (WNH) that explicitly considers all possible realizations 
of pipeline orders. Roughly speaking, the WNH approximation is a 
random-yield version of the myopic solution to the deterministic-yield 
setting in which the order quantity is determined by the gap between 
the target inventory level (same value as in the LIR) and the beginning 
inventory levels in the (𝐿+ 1)th period, where 𝐿 is the leadtime. 
This heuristic is derived based on a sample-path approach: for each 
realization of the pipeline orders, there is a corresponding gap, and it 
then takes a weighted average of these gaps, where the weights are 
the corresponding probabilities of the yield sample paths of pipeline 
orders (see Section 4.2 for more details). Clearly, the approximation 
takes advantage of the all-or-nothing yield pattern. This resulting policy 
performs well in their numerical simulations. However, this method 
is still a myopic solution and fails to account for the functional de-
pendence of these weights on the current inventory level and pipeline 
orders. Therefore, the current paper is motivated to improve upon these 
known heuristics.
The primary objective of this paper is to find an algorithm that 
can improve upon known heuristics. For this purpose, the first aim of 
this paper is to develop a new heuristic that takes advantage of the 
all-or-nothing yield pattern. We draw on the structure of WNH and 
propose a new heuristic that adopts the gradient descent algorithm 
to search the optimal state-independent weights with the objective 
of minimizing the total expected discounted cost over the planning 
horizon (see Section 4.3 for more details). We prove that the total cost 
to be computed is convex in the weight vector, which facilitates the 
search.
The second aim of this paper is to explore the potential of applying 
DRL techniques to learn an optimal ordering policy. Reinforcement 
learning (RL) can efficiently solve sequential decision-making prob-
lems, while deep neural network (DNN) can effectively circumvent the 
curse of dimensionality issue (Boute et al., 2022). By reformulating a 
problem as a Markov decision process (MDP) with state transitions, a 
DNN (or even a neural network, NN) can approximate action value 
or policy functions. Combining both RL and DNN gives rise to DRL. 
Then, the DRL agent can learn excellent strategies by taking actions in 
a trial-and-error way, interacting with the environment, and generat-
ing reward or punishment signals. In recent years, DRL has achieved 
impressive success in terms of finding winning strategies in games 
and beyond. Notable examples include deep model-free Q-learning 
(DQN) for classic Atari 2600 games (Mnih et al., 2015) and the Monte 
Carlo simulation policy combined with the search algorithm AlphaGo, 
which resulted in defeating top human experts in Go games (Silver 
et al., 2016). There have also been numerous exploratory studies 
from different aspects of the field of inventory management in recent 
years. For example, Gijsbrechts et al. (2022) demonstrate that the 
Asynchronous Advantage Actor Critic (A3C) algorithm can effectively 
solve three classic inventory problems: dual sourcing, lost sales, and 
multi-echelon inventory management. (Oroojlooyjadid et al., 2022) 
propose the shaped-reward DQN (SRDQN) algorithm, which learns 
a near-optimal policy in the classic Beer game problem. (De Moor 
et al., 2022) leverage domain knowledge from heuristics to improve 
the performance of deep Q-network algorithm in a perishable inven-
tory problem. Kaynov et al. (2024) apply a DRL solution based on 
a proposed random rationing policy for one-warehouse multi-retailer 
inventory management. Wang and Minner (2024) apply the proximal 
policy optimization (PPO) algorithm to address a demand fulfillment 
problem in online retailing with different distribution centers, which 
has also achieved promising outcome. Given the DRL algorithms’ ex-
cellent performance, this paper tries to apply DRL techniques to solve 
the inventory system with an all-or-nothing random yield pattern.
To achieve the second aim, this paper develops a customized DNN 
model that takes advantage of the particular structure of the yield 
pattern to output state-dependent weights. A DRL algorithm then im-
plements this DNN model as a policy function approximator to learn an 
ordering strategy to minimize the expected total discounted cost. This 
well-structured function approximator allows faster convergence when 
deploying the DRL algorithm. Furthermore, from the implementation 
perspective, the proposed DRL algorithm integrates the results from a 
known heuristic to improve DRL performance and stabilize the training 
process. Specifically, the known heuristic is fed into the new DRL 
algorithm as an initial solution. Through a reward-shaping mechanism, 
the DRL algorithm learns to improve the results from the heuristic.
Although the data used in our experiments is generated by sampling 
from chosen distributions for demand and yield rate over time, the pro-
posed two methods do not make any assumption on either distribution. 
In other words, the underlying methods are data-driven, which sets 
our paper apart from the existing literature on random yield inventory 
problems. So far, little is known about the optimal solution for this 
periodic review inventory policy with random yield and non-zero lead-
times. Through a relatively extensive numerical study, the proposed 
two methods have been evaluated against two known benchmark poli-
cies, and the results show these new methods outperform benchmarks 
at least with a 2% cost gap. When the leadtime is small, where exact 
DP numerical methods can be used to solve the Bellman equation, the 
minimal cost is achievable; in this case, we can obtain the (globally) 
minimal total discounted cost. Our methods have demonstrated near-
optimal performance compared to the minimal cost achieved by DP and 
better performance to other known policies.
The rest of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews 
the closely related literature. Section 3 describes the inventory model. 
Section 4 proposes a new heuristic and a DRL algorithm as solution 
methods. Section 5 reports the results of an intensive numerical study. 
Section 6 concludes the paper with a summary.
2. Literature review
Inventory models with random yield and stochastic demand have 
drawn attention from many researchers in the operations management 
community. We mainly focus our review on studies that investigate 
random yield models and relevant approximation methods. For ease of 
exposition, the related literature can be categorized into zero leadtime 
and non-zero leadtimes cases. For a comprehensive review, we refer 
to Yano and Lee (1995) for random yield risks and Inderfurth and 
Vogelgesang (2013) for the approximation methods.
For the case of zero leadtime, Henig and Gerchak (1990) provide a 
comprehensive analysis of the stochastically proportional yield model 
in a general periodic review production/inventory model, proving that 
the optimal inventory replenishment policy involves a time-dependent 
critical target stock level. According to this policy, orders are placed if 
the current inventory level does not exceed this target stock thresh-
old in a single supply source. Unlike a base stock rule used under 
deterministic yield conditions, this policy incorporates a non-linear

Due to its computational complexity and impracticality, a simple ap-
proximation approach is helpful. Several of these approaches, including 
the newsvendor and NLH-2 heuristic introduced by Bollapragada and 
Morton (1999) as well as the heuristic developed by Li et al. (2008), 
aim to approximate the optimal rule using a more straightforward non-
linear function. Alternatively, the order quantity for more practical 
policies (Huh and Nagarajan, 2010) follows a linear relationship of the 
deviation between the target stock level and the inventory position. 
This type of policy is known as LIR. This rule offers a practical and 
efficient approach for determining order quantities in an inventory 
system with a random yield model by simplifying the decision-making 
process and reducing computational effort. However, these methods 
mainly consider the zero leadtime setting. Federgruen and Yang (2011) 
generalize the model proposed by Henig and Gerchak (1990) to include 
multiple suppliers. In particular, they develop an efficient algorithm 
to find the optimal sourcing strategy from multiple suppliers with 
random yield and price characteristics. Li (2017) investigate optimal 
procurement strategies from two suppliers with random yield and all-
or-nothing risks. Our research is also related to the system disruption 
literature, in which random disruptions can be considered as a special 
case of the stochastically proportional yield model and are equivalent 
to the all-or-nothing yield pattern: the firm receives either 0% or 100% 
of the order (Wang et al., 2010; Tang et al., 2014). Random yield 
models have many applications in different aspects, including assembly 
system (DeCroix, 2013), different payment schemes (Kouvelis et al., 
2018), and supply chain finance (Yuan et al., 2021), but the focus is 
quite different from the main body of this work.
In the extensive literature on random yield models, a few published 
studies report results with specific heuristics for problems with positive 
leadtimes. For example, Inderfurth (2009) extend the parameter deter-
mination approach of Bollapragada and Morton (1999) and develop 
LIR for non-zero leadtimes cases. Inderfurth and Vogelgesang (2013) 
further consider a time-dependent critical target stock policy with 
LIR under stochastic demand and different types of yield randomness 
settings. They also present safety stock formulas and investigate the 
performance of the proposed safety stock variants in a numerical study. 
Based on the study of Inderfurth and Vogelgesang (2013), Inderfurth 
and Kiesmüller (2015) focus on the class of stationary linear infla-
tion policies and investigate a periodic-review inventory system with 
binomial and stochastically proportional two different yield models. 
Compared with a general form of yield randomness, Chen et al. (2018) 
consider a special case — the all-or-nothing production yield pattern 
with non-zero leadtimes. They take advantage of the unique form 
of yield randomness, explicitly consider the uncertainty of the yield 
in determining order quantities, and develop the WNH policy. (Bu 
et al., 2020) investigate constant-order policies for lost-sales inventory 
models with non-zero leadtimes and random yield, thereby proving 
that the best policy is asymptotically optimal with large leadtimes. Our 
study contributes to this stream of literature by proposing a new DRL 
algorithm, which is a general technique for sequential decision-making 
problems that shows better performance compared with LIR and WNH 
policies in the following numerical simulation section.
3. Model formulation
We consider a single-item, periodic-review inventory system with 
stochastic demand, an all-or-nothing random yield pattern and non-
zero leadtimes in a finite horizon. After each order is received, the 
firm knows the yield result.  In our earlier example, upon receiving 
the order, the firm immediately takes a sample test; if the sample test 
fails, the entire shipment is considered defective and either disposed of 
or returned. We also assume that random yields are independent across 
orders.

4. Methodology
In this section, we propose two new solutions to learn a well-
performing ordering policy for the inventory control problem with an 
all-or-nothing random yield pattern. First, we formalize this inventory 
problem as an MDP. Then, we introduce two known heuristic policies 
that serve as benchmarks for our new solutions. Finally, we propose a 
new heuristic with state-independent weights and a new DRL approach 
with state-dependent weights motivated by WNH.

benchmark, the loss function provides a clear and intuitive measure 
of how well the policy network has played relative to an established 
baseline (Oroojlooyjadid et al., 2022). On the other hand, the original 
cost is an absolute value and fluctuates greatly due to uncertainties 
from both the demand and production sides. If the absolute cost is used 
as the loss function, it would likely cause the computed gradient to 
fluctuate greatly when using the gradient descent algorithm to update 
the network’s parameters. This makes it difficult for the DRL algorithm 
to converge to the optimal solution.
Notably, our approach is different from the works of De Moor et al. 
(2022) and Oroojlooyjadid et al. (2022). De Moor et al. (2022) provide 
additional rewards at each time step for guiding the DQN agent using 
the potential-based shaped reward function proposed by Wiewiora et al. 
(2003). Oroojlooyjadid et al. (2022) use reward shaping via a feedback 
scheme in a multiagent and cooperative beer game, whose setting is 
not the focus of this paper.

5. Numerical experiments
We design two sets of experimental settings to perform a rigor-
ous performance analysis of the proposed two methods described in 
Sections 4.3 and 4.4 in the inventory management problem with an 
all-or-nothing random yield. The first set of experiments considers a 
small-scale setup where the DP model can be solved. We compare 
the proposed methods with the optimal solution obtained by exact 
numerical DP methods and two known benchmark policies mentioned 
in Section 4.2. The second set of experiments compares these new 
solutions to two known heuristics in large-size settings where the DP 
model is intractable.
Section 5.1 gives the implementation details. In Section 5.2, we test 
a set of numerical experiments selected from Chen et al. (2018) and 
show the near-optimality of our solutions. In Section 5.3, we design 
various numerical experiments with different demand distributions and 
cost values, after which we present the realized cost results under these 
experimental settings.
5.1. Implementation details
To train the proposed DRL algorithm, we first construct a fully 
connected multilayer neural network for the policy network consisting 
of three hidden layers. All hidden layers use the rectified linear unit 
(ReLU) activation function (Nair and Hinton, 2010) that introduces 
nonlinearity to capture complex relationships in the data. In this neural 
network, the number of nodes in the input layer is determined by the 
length of the state vector, resulting in 𝐿+ 1 input nodes. The number 
of nodes in the output layer is equal to the length of the weight vector; 
thus, we have 2𝐿−1 nodes in the output layer.
For each problem instance, we train a DRL agent according to 
the details described in Algorithm 1. The agent interacts with the 
environment to gather training data experiences, calculates the loss, 
and updates the network parameters using the Adam optimizer until 
the training stop condition is triggered. This study uses 500 episodes 
for training. Subsequently, the trained DRL agent with fixed parameters 
is then evaluated on 100 simulated random test trajectories of length 
𝑇, where every test trajectory is generated with a distinct random 
seed without reuse. This ensures complete independence across all 
training and evaluation data. The two benchmark policies and GDSH 
are similarly evaluated on the same test simulated trajectories. In 
addition, we conduct a manual hyperparameter tuning on the batch size 
and the range hyperparameter.  Specifically, we apply a batch gradient 
descent optimization method with an initial learning rate of 0.003 to 
update our network parameters. The batch size is thus equal to the size 
of the training dataset. The learning rate decreases exponentially with 
a decay factor of 0.98 after every epoch, and the minimum learning 
rate is 0.0001. In all the experiments with leadtime 𝐿= 2 and 𝐿=
3 (respectively, 𝐿= 5), the range hyperparameter 𝛿 is set to 0.25 
(respectively, 0.0884). Appendix A summarizes all the hyperparameter 
values for our numerical experiments when training the proposed DRL 
method.

6. Conclusion
This paper investigates a single-item, periodic-review inventory 
system with stochastic demand, an all-or-nothing random yield pattern, 
and non-zero leadtimes in a finite horizon where the unmet demand 
is backlogged. The firm aims to find an optimal ordering policy to 
minimize the expected total discounted cost over the planning horizon, 
including purchase, inventory holding, and shortage costs. However, 
the optimal policy remains unknown, and the corresponding struc-
ture is likely to be complicated due to the challenges intrinsic to an 
inventory problem with an all-or-nothing random yield model.
To solve this inventory problem, this paper first draws on the 
structure of an existing heuristic policy and proposes a new heuristic 
with state-independent weights. We prove that the total cost to be 
computed is convex in the weight vector, which facilitates the search. 
Moreover, we propose a new DRL algorithm with state-dependent 
weights based on a tailored neural network architecture. Furthermore, 
this algorithm combines the results of existing heuristics to improve 
performance and stabilize the training process. Extensive numerical 
studies demonstrate that our methods can achieve near-optimal per-
formance compared to the optimal DP solution and outperform two 
known benchmark policies, especially in cases with a moderate yield 
possibility. Given the computational burden of DRL, the new heuristic 
seems a well-performing method.
While the findings demonstrate that our methods can effectively

Appendix B. Ordering behavior analysis
Given that there is a total of 432 problem instances, we select 
two representative experiments to analyze the ordering strategies of 
heuristics, excluding cases where leadtime is equal to 5 from the visual 
analysis. Notably, the policy performance of each method depends on 
the characteristics of the specific problem instance, but the analysis 
patterns are similar.
Fig. B.1 visualizes the ordering behaviors of different policies in 
two experiments where demand follows a normal distribution and a 
gamma distribution, respectively. Fig. B.2 reports the increment of the 
corresponding order quantities for LIR and WNH policies compared 
with our DRL algorithm. We can observe that order quantities from 
LIR policy have a linear relationship with inventory levels and or-
der pipelines. However, the ordering strategy of our DRL algorithm 
presents a more complex relationship relative to current state variables 
due to the introduction of activation functions by neural networks to 
represent nonlinear relationships. Upon observing the red area in the 
lower left corner of Figs. B.1 and B.2, it is evident that LIR and WNH 
policies order more products than DRL, particularly when inventory

to a significant increase in the cost of LIR and WNH. In addition, for 
some states with high pipeline orders, WNH and LIR policies show a 
preference for fewer order quantities than DRL. This outcome may be 
attributed to these two benchmarks being overly optimistic about the 
arrival of products in transit. In contrast, there is a high probability 
that the latest shipment will be 0 if the sample test fails. Overall, DRL 
can more intelligently adjust order quantities by introducing nonlinear 
representations, ultimately reducing costs.

D.2. Non-independent and identically distributed (non-iid) demands
In the non-iid demand scenario, although the demand patterns can 
change over time, the corresponding DP formulation remains consistent 
with Eq. (1). We can train our DRL agent separately for each period 𝑡, 
ensuring that the random demand within the same period follows an 
independent and identical distribution (Mao et al., 2024).
Fig. D.1 shows the interaction process between our DRL agent 
and the environment in an episode under the non-iid demand case. 
Specifically, for each period 𝑡, a separate DRL agent with parameters 𝜃𝑡
processes the current state 𝑠𝑡 to determine the order quantity 𝑞𝑡. Each 
separate DRL agent has the same policy structure described in Fig. 2 
with different parameters 𝜃𝑡. The agent tailored at each period incurs 
a cost for its interactions with the environment. The total cost 𝐶𝑇 for 
each episode, is then used to calculate the loss function that updates 
each DRL agent. This method allows the DRL agent to be specifically 
tailored to the demand patterns of that period. The objective is to 
find the optimal parameter 𝜃∗
𝑡 of the policy network for each separate 
DRL agent to minimize the expected total discounted cumulative cost. 
Besides, we calculate the target inventory level 𝑆𝐿𝑡 for each period 𝑡
based on the approach outlined in Inderfurth and Kiesmüller (2015), 
noting that their approach is applicable only in the iid demand case.
